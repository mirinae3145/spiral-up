# Case analysis

Use this procedure for accumulated cases, selected observations, and assessments of earlier improvements.
Direct change requests enter the [Common update path](update.md) without needing this analysis.

## Establish the evidence

1. Resolve the selected records and their organization using [Records and storage](records.md).
   Scope the review to the user's question, selected cases, or relevant topic.
   Avoid an unrelated audit of every accessible store.
   Preserve original records and supporting material.
1. Identify what was expected, what was observed, the impact, recovery actions, and the current condition.
   Separate supported explanations from hypotheses and missing history.
   Treat a record's attribution, severity, and interpretation as claims to check against its cited evidence.
   Note each incident's severity and the agent and model that acted, assessing severity from the evidence when the record lacks it.
1. When relevant and available, identify the instruction or skill version in effect at the time, including authorized exceptions.
   Do not judge a past action solely against wording introduced after it occurred.
   If the applicable version is unknown, state the limit rather than reconstructing it.
1. Treat later reviews, copied summaries, and recovery updates about one incident as the same occurrence.
   Establish that relationship through existing identifiers and evidence references.
   Do not merge uncertain matches or invent occurrence counts.
   Compare separate incidents, useful outcomes, counterexamples, and observed mitigation results, distinguishing problems confined to one agent or model from those seen across them.
   Selective case collection supports comparison, not an overall failure rate or proof of a model-wide tendency.

## Choose the intervention

1. Determine whether the evidence points to unclear, conflicting, or misplaced guidance, failure to follow adequate guidance, or a tool or environment problem.
   Do not treat an unsupported diagnosis as an established explanation.
   Consider the proposed intervention's benefit, cost, intended audience, and smallest scope that addresses the evidence.

   An explicit user preference can justify a change without recurring cases.
   A causal hypothesis needs evidence appropriate to its uncertainty.
   Repeated incidents invite comparison rather than automatic rule creation.
1. Weigh severity alongside recurrence to set priority.
   A high-severity incident can justify prioritizing an improvement from a single case, such as a targeted safeguard against that impact.
   Keep that improvement proportionate and state its limited evidence; low-severity friction usually warrants comparison across cases first.
   When improvements compete, address higher-severity issues first unless the user sets other priorities.
1. Place the improvement for the agents and models it concerns.
   A problem confined to one model may call for agent-specific guidance or a tool fix rather than shared guidance.
   Do not generalize from one model's behavior to all agents without supporting evidence.
1. Choose a supported conclusion, such as a targeted guidance change, a new rule or skill, consolidation or removal, further checks, or no change.
   A tool fix may be appropriate but belongs in separate work.
   Make a supported improvement actionable while retaining material uncertainty and counterexamples.
   Pass its purpose, proposed scope, linked evidence, and uncertainties to the common update path.
   The conclusion does not itself authorize application.

## Reassess an earlier improvement

1. Connect the original cases, improvement decision, applied source version, and later observations when those references exist.
   Distinguish source application and installation from actual use of the changed guidance in the observed task.
   A repeated problem before installation is not evidence that the changed guidance failed during use.
   If exposure to the changed guidance is unknown, preserve that uncertainty.
1. Assess whether observations support the intended effect, reveal new friction, or remain inconclusive.
   Avoid attributing a result to the change when other explanations remain plausible, including a different agent or model.
   Preserve the original observation and decision.
   Append dated findings or linked assessments rather than rewriting earlier evidence to match the latest explanation.
